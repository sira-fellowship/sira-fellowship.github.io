In short

  • The field is large and growing fast. More than 100,000 social science papers on AI’s societal effects since 2015. They went from about 1 in 1,300 social science papers in 2019 to about 1 in 50 in 2025. See the growth
  • It is becoming more rigorous. The share using experiments, surveys, observational data, or fieldwork rose from 19% in 2019 to 43% in 2025. See the breakdown
  • The gaps are specific. Persuasion and manipulation is 2% of the field, though 30% of its papers are experiments. Economic and labor research is large but mostly reviews and commentary: only about a third uses data of any kind. See the gaps
  • Labs and governments are investing. Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI all run teams or programs on AI’s societal effects, and the UK has launched an AI Economics Institute. See who’s asking

Where social science fits

AI safety is broad, and social science does not cover all of it. Risks are commonly grouped into three families. We work on a deliberate slice of two of them.

Misalignment

Outside our scope

An AI system pursues something other than what its developers intended, as in reward hacking or deceptive behavior. This work needs deep machine learning expertise that the fellowship is not built to provide.

Misuse

Partly in scope

A person deliberately points a working system at harm, from cyberattacks to mass persuasion. We focus on persuasion and manipulation, where the harm runs through human psychology.

Structural risk

Largely in scope

Harm builds up from many actors’ choices with no single bad actor: concentrated power, labor disruption, eroding trust. We focus on its economic and institutional channels.

We also leave out state-level questions, such as AI races between countries and authoritarian surveillance. Within misuse and structural risk, we focus on four threat models where experiments, surveys, and field studies with real people give social scientists real leverage.

Why social science

Many of these harms run through people: what they believe, whom they trust, how they work, and how their institutions decide. Psychologists, cognitive scientists, economists, and sociologists already measure those things, using randomized experiments, large surveys, field studies, and administrative data.

That turns open questions about AI and society into findings that can be tested, replicated, and acted on by labs and policymakers. The examples below show what that looks like in practice.

Research on AI’s societal effects grew 17-fold in six years

Social science papers on AI’s effects on people, organizations, institutions, and economies, per year, by method.

Our census finds more than 100,000 such papers since 2015, rising from 1,837 in 2019 to 31,151 in 2025. This is not just publishing growth: social science output as a whole did not grow over the same years, so the share of social science papers on AI’s societal effects rose from about 1 in 1,300 in 2019 to about 1 in 50 in 2025. The work is also becoming more empirical: the share using experiments, surveys, observational data, or qualitative fieldwork rose from 19% in 2019 to 43% in 2025.

Four threat models, with real examples

For each threat model, two questions social scientists are already answering, and a study that answers them.

Psychology · Cognitive science

Human-AI interaction harms

Attachment to AI companions, over-reliance and automation bias, treating AI as human, and design that optimizes for engagement.

Does an AI companion make lonely people less lonely, or more isolated?

Chatbot companionship and loneliness · Liu, Pataranutaporn & Maes, MIT Media Lab, 2025

In a mixed-methods study of 404 regular companion-chatbot users, how much people used chatbots did not by itself predict loneliness. The researchers found seven distinct user profiles; companionship was linked to better well-being for some and worse for others.

Does a kind message still feel comforting once you learn an AI wrote it?

Comparing the value of perceived human versus AI-generated empathy · Rubin, Li, Zimmerman, Ong, Goldenberg & Perry, Nature Human Behaviour, 2025

Across nine studies with 6,282 participants, identical empathic replies were rated as less caring and supportive once people learned an AI had written them. Given the choice, people preferred to wait for a human reply over getting an immediate AI one.

Psychology · Communication

Misuse: persuasion and manipulation

Disinformation, influence operations, and how susceptible individuals are to AI-generated persuasion. The same persuasive power that can correct false beliefs can also be used to plant them.

Can a short conversation with an AI talk someone out of a conspiracy belief, and does it last?

Durably reducing conspiracy beliefs through dialogues with AI · Costello, Pennycook & Rand, Science, 2024

2,190 people who believed a conspiracy theory discussed it with GPT-4 Turbo, which offered personalized, evidence-based counterarguments. Belief fell by about 20%, and the effect was still there two months later.

Can AI tailor a persuasive message to someone’s personality, and does tailoring make it work better?

The potential of generative AI for personalized persuasion at scale · Matz, Teeny, Vaid, Peters, Harari & Cerf, Scientific Reports, 2024

Across four studies with 1,788 participants, messages that ChatGPT tailored to people’s psychological profiles were significantly more persuasive than generic ones. Personalized persuasion, once costly, can now be automated.

Economics

Structural risk: economic and labor channels

Labor-market disruption, market concentration among AI firms, why companies cut safety corners under competition, and who captures value and control as AI spreads.

Is ChatGPT changing who gets paid more, or who loses their job?

The (short-term) effects of large language models on unemployment and earnings · Chen, Kane, Kozlowski, Kunievsky & Evans, University of Chicago, 2025

Comparing occupations by how exposed they are to language models, before and after ChatGPT’s release, the authors found earnings rose in highly exposed occupations while unemployment did not change. So far, the adjustment has run through pay rather than job losses.

What do people using AI at work say it is doing to their jobs?

What 81,000 people told us about the economics of AI · Anthropic Economics team, 2026

Open-ended responses from about 80,500 Claude users. About one in five raised concerns about losing work, and that concern rose with how exposed a person’s occupation is to AI; early-career workers worried most. Respondents chose to take part, so they are not representative of all workers.

Sociology · Management

Structural risk: institutional and organizational channels

Eroding trust in institutions, organizational risk, uneven AI adoption across groups and institutions, and the decline of shared knowledge as a social process.

Do AI hiring tools discriminate by age and gender, even when no one designs them to?

Age and gender distortion in online media and large language models · Guilbeault, Delecourt & Desikan, Nature, 2025

Across about 1.4 million online images and videos and nine language models, women were portrayed as younger than men, most of all in high-status jobs. When ChatGPT generated and rated about 40,000 resumes, it assumed women were younger and less experienced, and rated older men as more qualified.

What happens to professional judgment when a company gives its workers AI?

Navigating the jagged technological frontier · Dell’Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon & Lakhani, Organization Science, 2026

In a randomized experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, those given GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at 40% higher quality on tasks within the AI’s abilities. On a task just beyond them, consultants using AI got the right answer less often than those without it: 60–70% against 84%.

Where the evidence is thin

Persuasion and manipulation has the least work so far: about 2,300 of the 104,000 papers in our census, or 2%, even though it is among the risks most directly measurable by experiment. Yet 30% of its papers are experiments, tied with human-AI interaction for the highest share of any area, so the method clearly works there; the field is short of researchers, not of tools. Economic and labor channels show a different gap: about 14,100 papers, but 58% are literature reviews or commentary, and only about a third use data of any kind, whether administrative and market data, surveys, or experiments. Empirical studies of AI’s economic effects, like the two highlighted above, are still the exception. Among our selected studies, economics dominates, with 99 items against 54 from psychology and 41 from sociology. These gaps are where new researchers can add the most.

    Who’s asking for this work

    Beyond the research itself, AI labs, governments, and universities are building teams and funding programs for exactly this kind of work, and AI safety researchers have been asking for social scientists for years.

    • AI labs. Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI each have dedicated teams, institutes, or programs studying AI’s economic and societal effects, and all five posted roles for economists and social-impact researchers in 2025–26.
    • Governments. The UK launched an AI Economics Institute in June 2026, and its AI Security Institute funds systemic safety research and runs a societal resilience program.
    • Universities. Stanford’s Digital Economy Lab, MIT Media Lab’s Advancing Humans with AI program, the Oxford Internet Institute, and the University of Virginia’s Economics of Transformative AI initiative all run dedicated research programs.
    • The AI safety community. Researchers have called for social scientists in AI safety since at least 2017, from Geoffrey Irving and Amanda Askell’s “AI Safety Needs Social Scientists” to recent posts on LessWrong and the EA Forum.

      How we built this

      We searched the publication pages of AI labs, government institutes, academic centers, and individual researchers, and checked the authorship and relevance of each item before adding it. Each item is tagged by threat model and discipline. Studies with large samples or randomized designs are flagged as large-N or RCT. Press releases and short news items are left out of the research list.

      This is a documented floor, not a census: there is certainly relevant work we have not found yet.

      The census behind the growth figures. We searched Semantic Scholar for papers in the social sciences (including economics, psychology, sociology, political science, business, law, and education) that pair AI terms with societal-impact terms, from 2015 through October 2026: about 139,000 candidates. Claude Haiku then classified each paper’s title and abstract against our definition: research on how AI affects people, organizations, institutions, economies, or politics, or how they perceive, use, or govern it, excluding papers that only use AI as a research tool. On a random sample of 183 papers, its labels agreed with a researcher’s hand labels 85% of the time (Cohen’s kappa 0.61). About 104,300 papers met the definition; adjusting for measured precision and recall gives about 101,200. As a check, the census found about a third of the academic papers in our selected studies; most of the rest are working papers or are indexed without an abstract, which keyword search cannot reach. Treat these figures as a lower bound. For the share of the field, we counted every paper Semantic Scholar assigns to the same social science fields in each year. That total dipped sharply in 2021 and 2022, most likely a change in indexing rather than a real decline, so the exact ratio should be read loosely; the direction is not in doubt. The code, search terms, classification instructions, and summary results are on GitHub.
